Methods

The toolkit applies wherever a model is expensive, the data are noisy, and decisions depend on knowing the uncertainty.

  • Decrease in computational cost Matching model detail to the data cut computation by about 40 times on real transient data. Gaussian process surrogates, which approximate the simulation and report their own uncertainty, combined with adaptive refinement cut it by about 150 times on a two-parameter benchmark. Accuracy was preserved in both cases.
  • Model selection The level of detail is chosen according to the quality of the data: the model is as accurate as the measurement error allows, and no more refined than that, so no computation is spent on detail the data cannot support.
  • Bayesian inference Parameters are estimated by combining prior knowledge with the data. The result is a probability distribution instead of a single value, so every estimate comes with credible intervals.
  • MCMC sampling Samplers such as Metropolis-Hastings and NUTS, compared on convergence, accuracy and computational cost. The comparison is published in the International Journal for Uncertainty Quantification.
  • Uncertainty quantification Parameter uncertainty is propagated to predictions, so every prediction comes with a credible interval.
  • Verification and validation Verification checks that the equations are solved correctly: my finite element solutions are compared with COMSOL, ANSYS and very fine reference solutions. Validation checks that the model describes reality: predictions are compared with real measurements, including experiments I built myself.
  • Optimization under uncertainty Searching for the best design (transducer position and exposure time) while the tissue properties are uncertain.

From experiment to calibrated model: temperature-dependent thermal conductivity

Key result. About 40 times less computation with 99% accuracy, on the transient dataset.

The experiment

I designed and built this prototype myself. It is 3D-printed around a column of paraffin wax. A brass plate at the bottom is heated, and a controller holds the plate at a set temperature. Styrofoam insulates the sides, and the top is open to the air. Four temperature sensors along the axis and one for the ambient air record the response for about 14 hours.

The model

The example is a nonlinear heat transfer problem. The thermal conductivity k(T) of the wax depends on temperature, which makes the problem nonlinear, and k(T) is the unknown. Heat conduction along the column, with losses through the sides, is described by the first equation. The boundary conditions describe the heated plate (x = 0) and the open top (x = L). The initial temperature is the ambient temperature.

The Bayesian part

Calibration is posed as Bayesian inference. The likelihood models the data from the temperature sensors: the simulated temperatures plus measurement noise. The prior models what we already know about the conductivity values. The posterior, explored with MCMC, returns the conductivity with credible intervals instead of a single curve.

P(p | d) ∝ P(p) P(d | p) Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood. p are the conductivity parameters and d the sensor data.

A model for the conductivity

To estimate a function, we first need a model for it. I use piecewise linear functions because they are easy to understand and to model. The number of segments sets the complexity: more segments allow a more detailed curve.

The algorithm

The algorithm chooses how fine the model needs to be: the number of finite elements, the number of time steps, and the number of conductivity segments. It refines only until the numerical error is comparable to the measurement noise, and adds segments only while information criteria say they are justified. It stops when the model is good enough for the data, instead of over-refining.

On the real data it adapted to the information in each dataset. With the full data (transient and steady state) it chose 24 elements, 512 time steps and 8 segments. With steady-state data only it chose 8 elements, 2 time steps and 2 segments.

Result

For the transient dataset, the algorithm used about 40 times less computation than a fixed fine setting, while preserving 99% accuracy: in a synthetic test, predictions deviated from a very fine reference by about 0.01%. In practice, calibration stops when more refinement no longer improves the answer, so computing time is not wasted.

The real-data run illustrates the algorithm: the other physical properties were calibrated beforehand and held fixed, and the model is one-dimensional.

Goal-oriented adaptive finite elements with Gaussian process surrogates

Key result. About 150 times faster Bayesian calibration on a problem with 2 unknowns, including the cost of building the surrogate. The surrogate reproduces the sensor temperatures to about 0.03%.

The problem

Find a hidden inclusion with lower thermal conductivity inside a block of material, using temperature readings. The block is heated at the bottom, insulated at the sides, and loses heat through the top, where four sensors measure temperature. The unknowns are the position, size and orientation of the inclusion.

Goal-oriented refinement

Bayesian calibration needs the simulation thousands of times, but only the sensor temperatures enter the likelihood. Goal-oriented refinement estimates the numerical error in those temperatures and refines the mesh only where it affects them. This is what the figure shows: refinement concentrates near the inclusion and the sensors.

Surrogate models

I train Gaussian process surrogates of the sensor temperatures on these inexpensive simulations. Each surrogate predicts a temperature and its own uncertainty, and that uncertainty is added to the likelihood. A less reliable surrogate therefore widens the result instead of biasing it. With 64 training simulations, the relative error is about 0.03%, and the predictive uncertainty is about three orders of magnitude smaller than the temperatures.

Results

Circular inclusion, 2 unknowns. About 150 times faster than finite element MCMC: about 20 s against about 3,100 s for 10,000 samples, including the training simulations.

Bayesian design of focused ultrasound tumor ablation

Key result. For a 1×5 mm tumor, one exposure of about 15 s gives a damage probability above 94% in the tumor and below 52% in the healthy tissue region. Larger tumors needed two to four sessions.

  • Design by stochastic simulations of the thermal ablation treatment of tumors with high intensity focused ultrasound, Numerical Heat Transfer, Part B: Fundamentals, 2024

The goal

Focused ultrasound can ablate a tumor without surgery by heating a small region. The goal is to find the ultrasound position and the exposure time that ablate the target location without considerably damaging the surrounding area, while accounting for the uncertainty of all the physical properties.

Acoustic model

Ultrasound propagation follows mass and momentum balance, with a nonlinear pressure and density relation and attenuation. I solved it with k-Wave, a MATLAB toolbox.

Bayesian design

The unknowns are the transducer position and the exposure time, together with the uncertain tissue properties. They are sampled with MCMC. The likelihood describes the desired outcome: near-certain damage in the tumor, an open margin around it, and low damage elsewhere. The sampler optimizes the design while the tissue properties only add uncertainty. Results

The thermal damage is concentrated in the tumor, with its mean on the left and its uncertainty on the right.

Splitting the domain into regions shows the intended pattern: the damage is large in the tumor, less aggressive in the surrounding area, and minimal further away.

The design variables were estimated with small uncertainty (about 0.1 mm and 0.1 s). Larger tumors needed two heating sessions (3×5 mm) and four (5×1 mm), and the sessions shift the focus across the tumor. In practice, the plan comes with a quantified reliability under uncertain tissue properties.

The tumors are idealized two-dimensional shapes. One single-session design took about 90 hours on a workstation, so computing cost is the main obstacle to practical use.
